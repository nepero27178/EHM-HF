\chapter{Introduction to Hartree-Fock methods}

In this chapter we give a general introduction to the Hartree-Fock (HF) variational method and its numerical implementation. In Quantum Mechanics, variational methods allow to approximate the ground state of a many-body hamiltonian by minimising the energy computed for a specific substate of physical states. In particular, in the HF method we approximate the real ground-state with the best possible many-body state that is an eigenstate of a non-interacting fermionic hamiltonian.

Despite its simplicity, the method is very powerful for describing various types of quantum many-body phenomena and, in particular, spontaneous symmetry breaking (SSB). The description of HF method in this chapter is largely based on \cite{grosso2014solid, fabrizio2022course}.

\section{Variational principle and gaussian states}

Let $\hat H$ be a generic hamiltonian acting on the Hilbert space $\mathcal{H}$. For any many-body state $\ket{\Psi} \in \mathcal{H}$, the energy is given by the expectation value (EV)
\[
	E[\Psi] = \mel{\Psi}{\hat H}{\Psi}
\]
Let $\ket{\Psi_0}$ be the ground state of $\hat H$, and $E_0 \equiv E[\Psi_0]$. Then
\[
	\forall \ket{\Psi} \in \mathcal{H}
	\quad\colon\quad
	E[\Psi] \ge E_0
\]
Let $\mathcal{S} \subset \mathcal{H}$ be a subspace of states, ad let $\ket{\Psi}$ be a state inside that locally minimises energy,
\[
	\ket{\Psi} \in \mathcal{S}
	\qquad
	\fdv{E[\Psi]}{\ket{\Psi}} = 0
\]
If the true ground state belongs to $\mathcal{S}$, the found local minimum coincides with $\ket{\Psi_0}$; otherwise, approximates it. In Fig.~\ref{fig:gaussian-subspace} we give a pictorial representation of this idea: the state $\ket{\Psi}\in\mathcal{S}$ is an approximation, found by constrained minimisation, of the true ground-state.

\begin{figure}
	\centering
	\input{\GraphicsFolder/gaussian-subspace}
	\caption{Pictorial representation of the search for the best approximation $\ket{\Psi}$ to the true ground-state inside the subspace $\mathcal{S}\subset\mathcal{H}$.}
	\label{fig:gaussian-subspace}
\end{figure}

In HF methods, we identify $\mathcal{S}$ with the set of so-called ``gaussian states''. These are all the ground-states of quadratic hamiltonians, namely the hamiltonians that are bilinear in the fermionic creations operators (see App.~\ref{appendix:slater-gaussian} for details). The restriction to gaussian states significantly simplifies the computation of expectation values. Indeed, thanks to Wick's theorem, the expectation value of quartic operators exactly decomposes as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hf-wick-hfc}
	\langle
	\hat c_\alpha^\dagger \hat c_\beta^\dagger \hat c_\gamma \hat c_\delta
	\rangle = \underbrace{
		\langle 
		\hat c_\alpha^\dagger \hat c_\delta
		\rangle \langle	
		\hat c_\beta^\dagger \hat c_\gamma 
		\rangle
	}_{\text{Hartree}}
	- 
	\underbrace{
		\langle 
		\hat c_\alpha^\dagger \hat c_\beta
		\rangle \langle	
		\hat c_\gamma^\dagger \hat c_\delta 
		\rangle 
	}_{\text{Fock}}
	+ 
	\underbrace{
		\langle 
		\hat c_\alpha^\dagger \hat c_\beta^\dagger
		\rangle \langle	
		\hat c_\gamma \hat c_\delta 
		\rangle 
	}_{\text{Cooper}}
\end{equation}
The subscripts indicate the standard names to the different types of contractions, that we adopt throughout the text.

%Let us rephrase: the HF method is a minimisation constrained to all states that satisfy exactly Eq.~\eqref{eq:hf-wick-hfc}. While this method simplifies a lot the computation of expectation values (reducing them to products of quadratic operators), the drawback is that the ground-state approximation is rather rough. Despite the roughness, HF methods are able to tackle various physical phenomena.

Operatively, one can think of the HF merhod as an approximation of the the hamiltonian with a non-interacting hamiltonian
\[
	\hat H \HFsimeq	\hat H_\mathrm{HF} = \sum_{\alpha} E_\alpha \hat\gamma_\alpha^\dagger \hat\gamma_\alpha
\]
%The notation $\HFsimeq$ indicates that this operatorial approximation only holds inside the subspace of gaussian states, and is explained rigorously in next paragraph. To determine the energies $E_\alpha$ as well as the fermionic operators $\hat\gamma_\alpha$ is the point of the HF method, and the result of minimisation procedure. These operators satisfy Fermi anti-commutation rules,
where $\hat \gamma_\alpha$ are fermionic operators determined by the minimisation procedure
\[
	\acomm{\hat\gamma_\alpha}{\hat\gamma_\beta^\dagger} = \delta_{\alpha\beta}
\]
and are connected to the conventional electronic operators $\hat c_\alpha$ through an unitary map. See App.~\ref{appendix:slater-gaussian} for details.

Let us stress that the operatorial approximation $\HFsimeq$ is a shorthand notation that must be intended as follows: $\hat H_\mathrm{HF}$ is that non-interacting operator whose ground-state is the best approximation to the ground-state of $\hat H$. This does not mean that in general the two operators are related.

\section{The HF variational method}\label{sec:sketch-hf-method}

We now dicuss the implementation of the variational HF method for the generic quartic hamiltonian:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hf-quartic-H}
	\hat H = \sum_{\alpha,\beta} A_{\alpha\beta} \hat c_\alpha^\dagger \hat c_\beta + \frac{1}{2} \sum_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta} B_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta} \hat c_\alpha^\dagger \hat c_\beta^\dagger \hat c_\gamma \hat c_\delta
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hf-AB-syms}
	A_{\alpha\beta} = A_{\beta\alpha}^*
	\qquad
	B_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta} = B_{\delta\gamma\beta\alpha}^*
\end{equation}
to ensure hermiticity. Moreover, the following equation must hold:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hf-B-sym}
	B_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta} = B_{\beta\alpha\delta\gamma}
\end{equation}
due to the property $\hat c_\alpha^\dagger \hat c_\beta^\dagger \hat c_\gamma \hat c_\delta = \hat c_\beta^\dagger \hat c_\alpha^\dagger \hat c_\delta \hat c_\gamma$, which follows from Fermi anticommutation rules.

Our aim is to approximate the above hamiltonian with a non-interacting hamiltonian. The most general quadratic hamiltonian is given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hf-Hhf-k}
	\hat H \simeq \hat H_\mathrm{HF} \equiv E_0 + \sum_{\alpha,\beta} k_{\alpha\beta} \hat\gamma_\alpha^\dagger \hat\gamma_\beta
\end{equation}
As is specified in Sec.~\ref{appsec:bogoliubov-valatin}, $\gamma$ and $c$ operators must be linked by an unitary map. To find the best approximation to the original hamiltonian means to identify:
\begin{itemize}
	\item the offset $E_0$;
	\item the fermionic operators $\hat \gamma_\alpha$, $\hat \gamma_\alpha^\dagger$;
	\item the $k_{\alpha\beta}$ coefficients. 
\end{itemize}
Note that, in terms of algebraic solution, $E_0$ is irrelevant. To determine it correctly is only relevant in order to get the correct approximation of the actual ground-state energy. Let us discuss briefly the features of $\hat H_\mathrm{HF}$ and then propose a solution.

\subsection{Properties of the target quadratic hamiltonian}

In this section we enumerate some useful properties of $\hat H_\mathrm{HF}$. We will use also the following notation:
\[
	\hat a_\alpha^- \equiv \hat c_\alpha
	\qquad
	\hat a_\alpha^+ \equiv \hat c_\alpha^\dagger
\]
Due to linearity of the unitary mapping, the operator $\hat H_\mathrm{HF}$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:hf-Hhf-k} can also be expressed as the most general quadratic hamiltonians for electronic operators:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hf-Hhf-h}
	\hat H_\mathrm{HF} = E_0 + \sum_{\alpha,\beta} \sum_{p,q} h_{\alpha\beta}^{pq} \hat a_\alpha^p \hat a_\beta^q
\end{equation}
By changing basis from $\hat \gamma$ to $\hat c$ operators, we moved all our ignorance into the new $h_{\alpha\beta}^{pq}$ matrix elements. These satisfy the relations:
\begin{align}
	h_{\alpha\beta}^{++} &= \left(h_{\beta\alpha}^{--}\right)^* \label{eq:hf-offdiag-relation} \\
	h_{\beta\alpha}^{+-} &= - h_{\alpha\beta}^{-+} \label{eq:hf-ondiag-relation}
\end{align}
Because of these properties, we actually need to determine just half of the fields, since the other half will be uniquely determined.

\begin{tabproof}[Symmetry properties of the tensor $h_{\alpha\beta}^{pq}$]
	
	Let us start by proving Eq.~\eqref{eq:hf-offdiag-relation}. The HF hamiltonian can be represented through Nambu spinors as
	\[
	\begin{aligned}
		\hat H_\mathrm{HF} &= E_0 + \hat \Phi^\dagger \mathbf{h} \hat \Phi \\
		&= E_0 + \begin{bmatrix}
			\hat{\mathbf{c}} \\ \hat{\mathbf{c}}^\dagger
		\end{bmatrix}^\dagger \begin{bmatrix}
			h^{+-} & h^{++} \\
			h^{--} & h^{-+}
		\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}
		\hat{\mathbf{c}} \\ \hat{\mathbf{c}}^\dagger
		\end{bmatrix}
	\end{aligned}
	\]
	Our hamiltonian must be hermitian. We conclude that:
	\[
		\hat H_\mathrm{HF}^\dagger = \hat H_\mathrm{HF}
		\quad\implies\quad
		h^{++} = \begin{bmatrix}
			h^{--}
		\end{bmatrix}^\dagger
	\]
	which reads, element-wise:
	\[
		h_{\alpha\beta}^{++} = \left(h_{\beta\alpha}^{--}\right)^*
	\]
	This proves Eq.~\eqref{eq:hf-offdiag-relation}.
	
	Eq.~\eqref{eq:hf-ondiag-relation} is proven as follows. We can set, without loss of generality,
	\[
		\Tr h^{+-} + \Tr h^{-+} = 0
	\]
	This passage is guaranteed by the fact that if $\mathbf{h}$ is not traceless, we can redefine it by subtracting its trace and including the latter in the energy shift $E_0$. Then
	\[
	\begin{aligned}
		\hat H_\mathrm{HF} &= E_0 + \sum_{\alpha,\beta} \left[
			h_{\alpha\beta}^{+-} \hat c_\alpha^\dagger \hat c_\beta + h_{\alpha\beta}^{-+} \hat c_\alpha \hat c_\beta^\dagger
		\right] + (\text{same sign terms}) \\
		&= E_0 + \sum_{\alpha,\beta} \left[
			h_{\alpha\beta}^{+-} \left(\delta_{\alpha\beta} - \hat c_\beta \hat c_\alpha^\dagger \vphantom{c_\beta^\dagger} \right) + h_{\alpha\beta}^{-+} \left(\delta_{\alpha\beta} - \hat c_\beta^\dagger \hat c_\alpha\right)
		\right] + (\text{same sign terms}) \\
		&= E_0 -\sum_{\alpha,\beta} \left[
			h_{\beta\alpha}^{+-} \hat c_\alpha \hat c_\beta^\dagger + h_{\beta\alpha}^{-+} \hat c_\alpha^\dagger \hat c_\beta
		\right] + (\text{same sign terms}) \\
	\end{aligned}
	\]
	In second row we got rid of the traces sum and exchanged indices $\alpha \leftrightarrow \beta$. Confronting last and first row we conclude:
	\[
		h_{\beta\alpha}^{+-} = - h_{\alpha\beta}^{-+}
	\]
	which proves Eq.~\eqref{eq:hf-ondiag-relation}.
\end{tabproof}
%
These properties were all we needed to get to the main argument of the method.

\subsection{Self-consistent variational solution}\label{subsec:hf-variational-solution}

This section gives the operational method to obtain the variational solution. We follow and extend the argument proposed by \citeauthor{fabrizio2022course} \cite{fabrizio2022course}. Let:
\[
	\Delta_{\alpha\beta}^{pq} \equiv \big\langle \hat a_\alpha^p \hat a_\beta^q \big\rangle
\]
thus, explicitly
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hf-Delta-explicit}
	\Delta_{\alpha\beta}^{+-} = \big\langle \hat c_\alpha^\dagger \hat c_\beta \big\rangle
	\qquad
	\Delta_{\alpha\beta}^{-+} = \big\langle \hat c_\alpha \hat c_\beta^\dagger \big\rangle
	\qquad
	\Delta_{\alpha\beta}^{++} = \big\langle \hat c_\alpha^\dagger \hat c_\beta^\dagger \big\rangle
	\qquad
	\Delta_{\alpha\beta}^{--} = \big\langle \hat c_\alpha \hat c_\beta \big\rangle
\end{equation}
The first two are ``normal densities''; the second two are ``anomalous densities''. The reason for such names is the fact that these expectation values are non-zero if total charge conservation is broken. Conjugation of the last two gives a symmetry relation similar to Eq.~\eqref{eq:hf-offdiag-relation}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hf-ss-Delta-conj}
	\left( \Delta_{\alpha\beta}^{++} \right)^\dagger = \Delta_{\beta\alpha}^{--}
\end{equation}
Here $\Delta_{\alpha\beta}^{pq}$ are functions of the fields $h_{\alpha\beta}^{pq}$. These quantities satisfy the equation:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hf-variational-h}
	\sum_{\alpha,\beta} \sum_{p,q} h_{\alpha\beta}^{pq} \pdv{\Delta_{\alpha\beta}^{pq}}{h_{\mu\nu}^{rs}} = 0
\end{equation}
Let us prove this.

\begin{tabproof}[Proof of Eq.~\eqref{eq:hf-variational-h}]
	
	The HF ground-state energy is given by:
	\[
	\begin{aligned}
		E_\mathrm{HF} &= \ev{\hat H_\mathrm{HF}} \\
		&= E_0 + \sum_{\alpha,\beta} \sum_{p,q} h_{\alpha\beta}^{pq} \Delta_{\alpha\beta}^{pq}
	\end{aligned}
	\]
	where the expectation value is taken over the (up to now unknown) HF ground-state. Derive it:
	\begin{align}
		\pdv{E_\mathrm{HF}}{h_{\mu\nu}^{rs}} &= \pdv{}{h_{\mu\nu}^{rs}} \sum_{\alpha,\beta} \sum_{p,q} h_{\alpha\beta}^{pq} \Delta_{\alpha\beta}^{pq} \nonumber \\
		&= \Delta_{\mu\nu}^{rs} + \sum_{\alpha,\beta} \sum_{p,q} h_{\alpha\beta}^{pq} \pdv{\Delta_{\alpha\beta}^{pq}}{h_{\mu\nu}^{rs}} \label{eq:hf-pdv(E)-1}
	\end{align}
	Note that by construction $E_\mathrm{HF}$ is greater than the actual ground-state energy, since our approximated wavefunction is not the actual ground-state wavefunction. From Hellman-Feynman theorem \cite{grosso2014solid}, we know that for a parametric hamiltonian it holds:
	\begin{align}
		\pdv{E_\mathrm{HF}}{h_{\mu\nu}^{rs}} &= \mel{\Psi}{\pdv{\hat H_\mathrm{HF}}{h_{\mu\nu}^{rs}}}{\Psi} \nonumber \\
		&= \mel{\Psi}{\pdv{}{h_{\mu\nu}^{rs}}\sum_{\alpha,\beta} \sum_{p,q} h_{\alpha\beta}^{pq} \hat a_\alpha^p \hat a_\beta^q}{\Psi} \nonumber \\
		&= \big\langle \hat a_\mu^r \hat a_\nu^s \big\rangle \nonumber \\
		&= \Delta_{\mu\nu}^{rs} \label{eq:hf-pdv(E)-2}
	\end{align}
	Eqns.\eqref{eq:hf-pdv(E)-1} and \eqref{eq:hf-pdv(E)-2}, combined, imply:
	\[
		\sum_{\alpha,\beta} \sum_{p,q} h_{\alpha\beta}^{pq} \pdv{\Delta_{\alpha\beta}^{pq}}{h_{\mu\nu}^{rs}} = 0
	\]
	which ends the proof.
\end{tabproof}

We now use Eq.~\eqref{eq:hf-variational-h} to minimise the variational energy $E = \langle \hat H \rangle$. Performing Wick's contractions, the variational energy becomes
\begin{align}
	E &= \big\langle \hat H \big\rangle \nonumber \\
	&= \sum_{\alpha,\beta} A_{\alpha\beta} \big\langle \hat c_\alpha^\dagger \hat c_\beta \big\rangle + \frac{1}{2} \sum_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta} B_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta} \big\langle \hat c_\alpha^\dagger \hat c_\beta^\dagger \hat c_\gamma \hat c_\delta \big\rangle \nonumber \\
	&= \sum_{\alpha,\beta} A_{\alpha\beta} \big\langle \hat a_\alpha^+ \hat a_\beta^- \big\rangle + \frac{1}{2} \sum_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta} B_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta} \left(
		\big\langle \hat a_\alpha^+ \hat a_\beta^+ \big\rangle \big\langle \hat a_\gamma^- \hat a_\delta^- \big\rangle - \big\langle \hat a_\alpha^+ \hat a_\gamma^- \big\rangle \big\langle \hat a_\beta^+ \hat a_\delta^- \big\rangle + \big\langle \hat a_\alpha^+ \hat a_\delta^- \big\rangle \big\langle \hat a_\beta^+ \hat a_\gamma^- \big\rangle
	\right) \nonumber \\
	&= \sum_{\alpha,\beta} A_{\alpha\beta} \Delta_{\alpha\beta}^{+-} + \frac{1}{2} \sum_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta} B_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta} \left(
		\Delta_{\alpha\beta}^{++} \Delta_{\gamma\delta}^{--} - \Delta_{\alpha\gamma}^{+-} \Delta_{\beta\delta}^{+-} + \Delta_{\alpha\delta}^{+-} \Delta_{\beta\gamma}^{+-}
	\right) \nonumber \\
	&= \sum_{\alpha,\beta} A_{\alpha\beta} \Delta_{\alpha\beta}^{+-}
	+ \frac{1}{2} \sum_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta} \left(
		B_{\alpha\gamma\delta\beta} - B_{\alpha\gamma\beta\delta}
	\right) \Delta_{\alpha\beta}^{+-} \Delta_{\gamma\delta}^{+-} 
	+ \frac{1}{2} \sum_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta} B_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta} \Delta_{\alpha\beta}^{++} \Delta_{\gamma\delta}^{--} \label{eq:E=<H>}
\end{align}
where in the second line, we inserted the relations \eqref{eq:hf-Delta-explicit}. Our argument is completely contained in the following statement: the HF approximation to the real ground state it obtained as the ground state of a quadratic hamiltonian whose fields $h_{\alpha\beta}^{pq}$ satisfy the following relations:
\begin{align}
	h_{\alpha\beta}^{+-} &= \frac{1}{2} \left[
	A_{\alpha\beta} + \sum_{\gamma,\delta}
	\left(
	B_{\alpha\gamma\delta\beta} - B_{\alpha\gamma\beta\delta}
	\right) \Delta_{\gamma\delta}^{+-} 
	\right] \label{eq:hf-solution-h+-} \\
	h_{\alpha\beta}^{++} &= \sum_{\gamma,\delta} \frac{B_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta} \Delta_{\gamma\delta}^{--}}{2} \label{eq:hf-solution-h++}
\end{align}
These equations, operationally, give the instructions to build the best possible fermionic non-interacting hamiltonian to reproduce our original hamiltonian. These equations are proven as follows:

\begin{tabproof}[Proof of Eqns.~\eqref{eq:hf-solution-h+-}, \eqref{eq:hf-solution-h++}]

	Consider $E$ as expressed in Eq.~\eqref{eq:E=<H>}. Upon taking the derivative, we obtain
	\begin{multline*}
		\pdv{E}{h_{\mu\nu}^{rs}} = \sum_{\alpha,\beta} A_{\alpha\beta} \pdv{\Delta_{\alpha\beta}^{+-}}{h_{\mu\nu}^{rs}} + \frac{1}{2} \sum_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta} \left(
			B_{\alpha\gamma\delta\beta} - B_{\alpha\gamma\beta\delta}
		\right) \left(
			\pdv{\Delta_{\alpha\beta}^{+-}}{h_{\mu\nu}^{rs}} \Delta_{\gamma\delta}^{+-} + \Delta_{\alpha\beta}^{+-} \pdv{\Delta_{\gamma\delta}^{+-}}{h_{\mu\nu}^{rs}}
		\right) \\
		+ \frac{1}{2} \sum_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta} B_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta} \left(
			\pdv{\Delta_{\alpha\beta}^{++}}{h_{\mu\nu}^{rs}} \Delta_{\gamma\delta}^{--} + \Delta_{\alpha\beta}^{++} \pdv{\Delta_{\gamma\delta}^{--}}{h_{\mu\nu}^{rs}} 
		\right)
	\end{multline*}
	which, by using Eq.~\eqref{eq:hf-B-sym}, becomes:
	\begin{multline*}
		\pdv{E}{h_{\mu\nu}^{rs}} = \sum_{\alpha,\beta} \left[
			A_{\alpha\beta} + \sum_{\gamma,\delta}
			\left(
				B_{\alpha\gamma\delta\beta} - B_{\alpha\gamma\beta\delta}
			\right) \Delta_{\gamma\delta}^{+-} 
		\right] \pdv{\Delta_{\alpha\beta}^{+-}}{h_{\mu\nu}^{rs}} \\
		+ \sum_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta} \frac{B_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}
		\Delta_{\gamma\delta}^{--}}{2} \pdv{\Delta_{\alpha\beta}^{++}}{h_{\mu\nu}^{rs}} + \sum_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta} \frac{B_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta} \Delta_{\alpha\beta}^{++}}{2} \pdv{\Delta_{\gamma\delta}^{--}}{h_{\mu\nu}^{rs}} 
	\end{multline*}
	Let us define
	\begin{equation}\label{eq:hf-CD-definition}
		C_{\alpha\beta} \equiv 
		\frac{1}{2} \left[
			A_{\alpha\beta} + \sum_{\gamma,\delta}
			\left(
				B_{\alpha\gamma\delta\beta} - B_{\alpha\gamma\beta\delta}
			\right) \Delta_{\gamma\delta}^{+-} 
		\right]
		\qquad
		D_{\alpha\beta} \equiv \sum_{\gamma,\delta} \frac{B_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta} \Delta_{\gamma\delta}^{--}}{2}
	\end{equation}
	From Fermi anticommutation rules, we have
	\[
		\Delta_{\alpha\beta}^{+-} = \delta_{\alpha\beta} - \Delta_{\beta\alpha}^{-+}
		\quad\implies\quad
		\pdv{\Delta_{\alpha\beta}^{+-}}{h_{\mu\nu}^{rs}} = -\pdv{\Delta_{\beta\alpha}^{-+}}{h_{\mu\nu}^{rs}}
	\]
	which implies
	\[
	\begin{aligned}
		\sum_{\alpha,\beta} 2C_{\alpha\beta} \pdv{\Delta_{\alpha\beta}^{+-}}{h_{\mu\nu}^{rs}} &= \sum_{\alpha,\beta} \left( C_{\alpha\beta} \pdv{\Delta_{\alpha\beta}^{+-}}{h_{\mu\nu}^{rs}} + C_{\alpha\beta} \pdv{\Delta_{\alpha\beta}^{+-}}{h_{\mu\nu}^{rs}} \right) \\
		&= \sum_{\alpha,\beta} \left( C_{\alpha\beta} \pdv{\Delta_{\alpha\beta}^{+-}}{h_{\mu\nu}^{rs}} - C_{\beta\alpha} \pdv{\Delta_{\alpha\beta}^{-+}}{h_{\mu\nu}^{rs}} \right)
	\end{aligned}
	\]
	Moreover, using Eqns.~\eqref{eq:hf-AB-syms}, \eqref{eq:hf-ss-Delta-conj},
	\[
		\sum_{\alpha,\beta} \frac{B_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta} \Delta_{\alpha\beta}^{++}}{2}
		= \sum_{\alpha,\beta} \left(
			\frac{B_{\delta\gamma\beta\alpha} \Delta_{\beta\alpha}^{--}}{2}
		\right)^*
	\]
	and, recognising $D_{\gamma\delta}$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:hf-CD-definition}, we conclude
	\[
		\sum_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta} \frac{B_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta} \Delta_{\alpha\beta}^{++}}{2} \pdv{\Delta_{\gamma\delta}^{--}}{h_{\mu\nu}^{rs}}
		= \sum_{\gamma,\delta}
		D_{\delta\gamma}^*
		\pdv{\Delta_{\gamma\delta}^{--}}{h_{\mu\nu}^{rs}}
	\]
	
	Recollect everything and impose the solution to be a stationary point of energy,
	\[
		\pdv{E}{h_{\mu\nu}^{rs}} \stackrel{!}{=} 0
	\]
	The notation ``$\stackrel{!}{=}$'' indicates, throughout the thesis, that we are imposing some condition. In the end we have:
	\begin{equation}\label{eq:hf-variational-CD}
		\pdv{E}{h_{\mu\nu}^{rs}} = \sum_{\alpha,\beta} \left( 
			C_{\alpha\beta} \pdv{\Delta_{\alpha\beta}^{+-}}{h_{\mu\nu}^{rs}} - C_{\beta\alpha} \pdv{\Delta_{\alpha\beta}^{-+}}{h_{\mu\nu}^{rs}} + D_{\alpha\beta} \pdv{\Delta_{\alpha\beta}^{++}}{h_{\mu\nu}^{rs}} + D_{\beta\alpha}^* 	\pdv{\Delta_{\alpha\beta}^{--}}{h_{\mu\nu}^{rs}}
		\right) \Ieq 0
	\end{equation}
	Comparing this equation with Eq.~\eqref{eq:hf-variational-h}. It must hold:
	\[
		h_{\alpha\beta}^{+-} = C_{\alpha\beta}
		\qquad
		h_{\alpha\beta}^{++} = D_{\alpha\beta} 
	\]
	Recalling their explicit form from Eq.~\eqref{eq:hf-CD-definition}, we prove Eqns.~\eqref{eq:hf-solution-h+-}, \eqref{eq:hf-solution-h++}.
\end{tabproof}

We notice that the right-hand side of both Eqns.~\eqref{eq:hf-solution-h+-}, \eqref{eq:hf-solution-h++} depends on the fields themselves, as $\Delta_{\alpha\beta}^{pq}$ are defined as expectation values of two-points fermionic operators over the ground-state $\ket{\Psi}$. For a given set of fields $h_{\alpha\beta}^{pq}$, the latter is determined (up to some unitary transformation) as the ground-state of a fermionic non-interacting model. In other words, being $\mathbf{h}$ the tensor containing all fields,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hf-self-consistency-general}
	\Delta_{\alpha\beta}^{pq}[\mathbf{h}] = \mel{\Psi[\mathbf{h}]}{\hat a_\alpha^p \hat a_\beta^q}{\Psi[\mathbf{h}]}
\end{equation}
The three Eqns.~\eqref{eq:hf-solution-h+-}, \eqref{eq:hf-solution-h++} and \eqref{eq:hf-self-consistency-general} define the solution of the HF variational method. Specifically, we need to determine $\Delta_{\alpha\beta}^{pq}$ such that, for given fields, Eq.~\eqref{eq:hf-self-consistency-general} is respected.

The list of parameters $\lbrace \Delta_{\alpha\beta}^{pq} \rbrace$ to be determined self-consistently may be not that short: by looking at Eq.~\eqref{eq:hf-self-consistency-general}, the indices $\alpha,\beta,p,q$ are spanning a large Hilbert space. However, this equation can be manipulated and transformed, reducing the set of parameters to be determined self-consistently to a very manageable number. For each phase we define these last as ``Hartree-Fock parameters'' (HFPs): a set of objects self-consistently determined by a set equivalent, but transformed, version of Eq.~\eqref{eq:hf-self-consistency-general}.

The great advantage of this scheme is that, with an appropriate choice of the Hilbert space basis (for example, moving to reciprocal space for a translationally-invariant model), we can implement easily model symmetries.

\subsection{Operative hamiltonian approximation}

In this section we give an alternative expression for Eqns.~\eqref{eq:hf-solution-h+-}, \eqref{eq:hf-solution-h++}, by using an equivalent (but more straightforward) operatorial approximation. For a generic two-body quartic operator, the HF approximation is given by the decomposition
\[
\begin{aligned}
	\hat c_\alpha^\dagger \hat c_\beta^\dagger \hat c_\beta \hat c_\alpha \HFsimeq &\hat c_\alpha^\dagger \hat c_\beta^\dagger \big\langle \hat c_\beta \hat c_\alpha \big\rangle + \big\langle \hat c_\alpha^\dagger \hat c_\beta^\dagger \big\rangle \hat c_\beta \hat c_\alpha - \big\langle \hat c_\alpha^\dagger \hat c_\beta^\dagger \big\rangle \big\langle \hat c_\beta \hat c_\alpha \big\rangle \quad&&(\text{Hartree})\\
	- &\hat c_\alpha^\dagger \hat c_\beta \big\langle \hat c_\beta^\dagger \hat c_\alpha \big\rangle - \big\langle \hat c_\alpha^\dagger \hat c_\beta \big\rangle \hat c_\beta^\dagger \hat c_\alpha + \big\langle \hat c_\alpha^\dagger \hat c_\beta \big\rangle \big\langle \hat c_\beta^\dagger \hat c_\alpha \big\rangle \quad&&(\text{Fock})\\
	+ &\hat c_\alpha^\dagger \hat c_\alpha \big\langle \hat c_\beta^\dagger \hat c_\beta \big\rangle + \big\langle \hat c_\alpha^\dagger \hat c_\alpha \big\rangle \hat c_\beta^\dagger \hat c_\beta - \big\langle \hat c_\alpha^\dagger \hat c_\alpha \big\rangle \big\langle \hat c_\beta^\dagger \hat c_\beta \big\rangle \quad&&(\text{Cooper})
\end{aligned}
\]
The proof of this statement is given in App.~\ref{appendix:hf-mft}, where we also argue the interpretation of HF method as a mean-field theory (MFT). Let us summarise this section through a practical rule to decompose the quartic part of the local repulsion $\hat H_U$ and the non-local attraction $\hat H_V$ in the EHM, by applying Eq.~\eqref{appeq:mft-V-decomposition}.

\paragraph{Local repulsion.} Applying the HF method, the local repulsion $\hat H_U$ is approximated by the quadratic operator:
\begin{align}
	\hat H_U &= U \sum_i \hat n_{i\uparrow} \hat n_{i\downarrow} \label{eq:mft-HU} \\
	&\HFsimeq U \sum_i \Big(
	\hat c_{i\uparrow}^\dagger \hat c_{i\uparrow} \big\langle \hat c_{i\downarrow}^\dagger \hat c_{i\downarrow} \big\rangle + \big\langle \hat c_{i\uparrow}^\dagger \hat c_{i\uparrow} \big\rangle \hat c_{i\downarrow}^\dagger \hat c_{i\downarrow} - \big\langle \hat c_{i\uparrow}^\dagger \hat c_{i\uparrow} \big\rangle  \big\langle \hat c_{i\downarrow}^\dagger \hat c_{i\downarrow} \big\rangle &&(\text{Hartree}) \nonumber\\
	&\phantom{\simeq \sum_{{i\uparrow}\neq{i\downarrow}} V_{{i\uparrow}{i\downarrow}}}
	- \hat c_{i\uparrow}^\dagger \hat c_{i\downarrow} \big\langle \hat c_{i\downarrow}^\dagger \hat c_{i\uparrow} \big\rangle - \big\langle \hat c_{i\uparrow}^\dagger \hat c_{i\downarrow} \big\rangle \hat c_{i\downarrow}^\dagger \hat c_{i\uparrow} + \big\langle \hat c_{i\uparrow}^\dagger \hat c_{i\downarrow} \big\rangle  \big\langle \hat c_{i\downarrow}^\dagger \hat c_{i\uparrow} \big\rangle &&(\text{Fock}) \nonumber\\
	&\phantom{\simeq \sum_{{i\uparrow}\neq{i\downarrow}} V_{{i\uparrow}{i\downarrow}}}
	+ \hat c_{i\uparrow}^\dagger \hat c_{i\downarrow}^\dagger \big\langle \hat c_{i\downarrow} \hat c_{i\uparrow} \big\rangle + \big\langle \hat c_{i\uparrow}^\dagger \hat c_{i\downarrow}^\dagger \big\rangle \hat c_{i\downarrow} \hat c_{i\uparrow} - \big\langle \hat c_{i\uparrow}^\dagger \hat c_{i\downarrow}^\dagger \big\rangle  \big\langle \hat c_{i\downarrow} \hat c_{i\uparrow} \big\rangle
	\Big) &&(\text{Cooper}) \nonumber
\end{align}

\paragraph{Non-local attraction.} Applying the HF method, the local repulsion $\hat H_V$ is approximated by the quadratic operator:
\begin{align}
	\hat H_V &= -V \sum_{\ev{ij}} \sum_{\sigma,\sigma'} \hat n_{i\sigma} \hat n_{j\sigma'} \label{eq:mft-HV} \\
	&\HFsimeq -V \sum_{\ev{ij}} \sum_{\sigma,\sigma'} \Big(
	\hat c_{i\sigma}^\dagger \hat c_{i\sigma} \big\langle \hat c_{j\sigma'}^\dagger \hat c_{j\sigma'} \big\rangle + \big\langle \hat c_{i\sigma}^\dagger \hat c_{i\sigma} \big\rangle \hat c_{j\sigma'}^\dagger \hat c_{j\sigma'} - \big\langle \hat c_{i\sigma}^\dagger \hat c_{i\sigma} \big\rangle  \big\langle \hat c_{j\sigma'}^\dagger \hat c_{j\sigma'} \big\rangle &&(\text{Hartree}) \nonumber\\
	&\phantom{\simeq \sum_{{i\sigma}\neq{j\sigma'}} V_{{i\sigma}{j\sigma'}}}
	- \hat c_{i\sigma}^\dagger \hat c_{j\sigma'} \big\langle \hat c_{j\sigma'}^\dagger \hat c_{i\sigma} \big\rangle - \big\langle \hat c_{i\sigma}^\dagger \hat c_{j\sigma'} \big\rangle \hat c_{j\sigma'}^\dagger \hat c_{i\sigma} + \big\langle \hat c_{i\sigma}^\dagger \hat c_{j\sigma'} \big\rangle  \big\langle \hat c_{j\sigma'}^\dagger \hat c_{i\sigma} \big\rangle &&(\text{Fock}) \nonumber\\
	&\phantom{\simeq \sum_{{i\sigma}\neq{j\sigma'}} V_{{i\sigma}{j\sigma'}}}
	+ \hat c_{i\sigma}^\dagger \hat c_{j\sigma'}^\dagger \big\langle \hat c_{j\sigma'} \hat c_{i\sigma} \big\rangle + \big\langle \hat c_{i\sigma}^\dagger \hat c_{j\sigma'}^\dagger \big\rangle \hat c_{j\sigma'} \hat c_{i\sigma} - \big\langle \hat c_{i\sigma}^\dagger \hat c_{j\sigma'}^\dagger \big\rangle  \big\langle \hat c_{j\sigma'} \hat c_{i\sigma} \big\rangle
	\Big) &&(\text{Cooper}) \nonumber
\end{align}
We will use these decomposition referring to each sector in dedicated chapters.

\section{The iHF algorithm}\label{sec:sketch-ihf-algorithm}

In this section we describe the algorithm we used to compute Eq.~\eqref{eq:hf-self-consistency-general}. We refer to this technique as ``iterative Hartree Fock'' (iHF). Let $\mathbf{v}$ be the vector containing all HFPs. Suppose we have $N$ HFPs $v_i$
\[
	\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{C}^N
	\qquad
	\mathbf{v} \equiv \begin{bmatrix}
		v_1 \\ v_2 \\ \vdots \\ v_N
	\end{bmatrix}
\]
In order to get a physical solution, each HFP must satisfy a self-consistency equation similar to Eq.~\eqref{eq:hf-self-consistency-general}. Each equation has the structure:
\[
	v_i = \text{(Some non-linear function of $\mathbf{v}$)}
\]
Let $\mathrm{A}$ be the non-linear operator collecting all these functions. The problem of finding a self-consistent solution can be reformulated as follows: $\mathbf{v}$ represents a physical solution if it is a fixed point of the map $\mathrm{A}$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hf-A-fixed-point}
	\mathrm{A} \colon \mathbb{C}^N \to \mathbb{C}^N
	\qquad
	\mathrm{A} (\mathbf{v}) = \mathbf{v}
	\quad\implies\quad
	\text{$\mathbf{v}$ is a solution.}
\end{equation}
We implement the search for a self-consistent solution as follows: first, we obtain an analytic expression for $\mathrm{A}$. Then we choose an initialiser $\mathbf{v}_0$ and apply $\mathrm{A}$ to it, obtaining a new HFPs vector. Iterating this procedure, we ``follow'' the non-linear map $\mathrm{A}$ until we converge.
%
\begin{figure}
	\centering
	\input{\GraphicsFolder/effective-model-scheme}
	\caption{Schematisation of the self-consistent Hartree Fock method described in Sec.~\ref{sec:sketch-ihf-algorithm}. The effective model is solved, the solution is used to estimate self-consistently the Hartree Fock vector $\mathbf{v}$, which is then used to update iteratively the model itself. The notation $\mathbf{v}_0$ indicates the initialiser HFPs vector, $\mathbf{v}_\mathrm{c}$ the converged HFPs vector.}
	\label{fig:effective-model-scheme}
\end{figure}
%
Specifically:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item We start with a generic quartic hamiltonian:
	\[
		\hat H = \sum_{\alpha\beta} A_{\alpha\beta} \hat c_\alpha^\dagger \hat c_\beta + \sum_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta} B_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta} \hat c_\alpha^\dagger \hat c_\beta^\dagger \hat c_\gamma \hat c_\delta
	\]
	We choose the model parameters and set the electronic density target;
	%
	\item Following the discussion of Sec.~\ref{sec:sketch-hf-method}, we approximate analytically $\hat H$ with a quadratic hamiltonian
	\[
		\hat H \simeq \hat H_\mathrm{HF} \equiv \sum_{\alpha\beta} \left[
			h_{\alpha\beta}^{+-} \hat c_\alpha^\dagger \hat c_\beta - h_{\beta\alpha}^{+-} \hat c_\alpha \hat c_\beta^\dagger + 			h_{\alpha\beta}^{++} \hat c_\alpha^\dagger \hat c_\beta^\dagger + \left(h_{\beta\alpha}^{++}\right)^* \hat c_\alpha \hat c_\beta
		\right]
	\]
	with the fields given by Eqns.~\eqref{eq:hf-solution-h+-}, \eqref{eq:hf-solution-h++};
	\item We impose selection rules on the fields $h_{\alpha\beta}^{+-}$, $h_{\alpha\beta}^{++}$, eventually eliminating those not respecting the symmetry sector we are targeting. For example, if we choose not to break total charge conservation, set $\forall\alpha,\beta \colon h_{\alpha\beta}^{++}=0$;
	\item We choose a starting value for the HFPs vector $\mathbf{v}_0$;
	\item We set $\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}_0$ and enter the iterative scheme of Fig.~\ref{fig:effective-model-scheme}:
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item Get a numeric approximation of the hamiltonian for the current value of $\mathbf{v}$, $\hat H_\mathrm{HF}[\mathbf{v}]$;
		\item Determine the value $\mu [\mathbf{v}]$ giving the desired density;
		\item Diagonalise the effective hamiltonian $\hat H_\mathrm{HF} [\mathbf{v}] - \mu[\mathbf{v}] \hat N$, and obtain the approximated ground-state $\ket{\Psi [\mathbf{v}]}$;
		\item Compute $\mathrm{A}(\mathbf{v})$ using self-consistency equations;
		\item If the algorithm converged, exit; otherwise, update the value of $\mathbf{v}$ repeat from point a).
	\end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}
In the following subsections we describe technical details concerning the chemical potential fixing and the convergence criteria used for the practical implementation of the above iterative scheme.

\subsection{Fixed density simulations and the role of $\mu$}\label{subsec:fixed-density-role-mu}

In this thesis all derivations and simulations are carried out at fixed target density. This is done by fixing the chemical potential $\mu$ during iterations of the algorithm. This must be done iteratively: changing at each step the HFPs vector, we are actively modifying the approximate hamiltonian. Chemical potential must be determined accordingly to maintain the required density. 

For a given choice of HFPs vector and chemical potential, the ground state $\ket{\Psi[\mathbf{v},\mu]}$ is uniquely determined; then we compute density as
\[
	n(\mathbf{v},\mu) \equiv \frac{1}{\mathcal{V}} \sum_{\alpha} \mel{\Psi[\mathbf{v},\mu]}{\hat c_\alpha^\dagger \hat c_\alpha}{\Psi[\mathbf{v},\mu]}
\]
being $\mathcal{V}$ the volume. Suppose we fix density to a value $n_0\in[0,1]$. At step $i$, naming the current HFPs vector $\mathbf{v}_i$, $\mu_i$ (the chemical potential at current step) is determined as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mu-numerical-minimization}
	\mu_i \equiv
	\min_\mu \left|
	n(\mathbf{v}_i,\mu) - n_0
	\right|
\end{equation}
The density $n$ must be a monotonically increasing function of $\mu$: then we can translate the above equation, which would inspire a minimisation procedure, to a much simpler node-search rule:
\[
	\mu_i = \mu \mid_{\delta n(\mathbf{v}_i,\mu) = 0}
	\qq{being}
	\delta n(\mathbf{v}_i,\mu) \equiv n(\mathbf{v}_i,\mu) - n_0
\]
In order to where the function changes sign (which is, the position of $\mu_i$) we use a basic bisection algorithm:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item Set an arbitrary tolerance $\Delta n > 0$ and a shifting parameter $\Delta \mu > 0$;
	\item Choose starting lower and upper bounds $\mu_\mathrm{L}<\mu_\mathrm{U}$;
	\item Compute $\delta n(\mathbf{v}_i,\mu_\mathrm{L})$. Evaluate: 
	\begin{itemize}
		\item if $\abs{\delta n(\mathbf{v}_i,\mu_\mathrm{L})} \le \Delta n$, set $\mu_i = \mu_\mathrm{L}$ and halt;
		\item if $\delta n(\mathbf{v}_i,\mu_\mathrm{L}) < -\Delta n$ do nothing;
		\item if $\delta n(\mathbf{v}_i,\mu_\mathrm{L}) > \Delta n$, shift the lower bound down, $\mu_\mathrm{L} \to \mu_\mathrm{L} - \Delta \mu$ and repeat from step 3;
	\end{itemize}
	\item Compute $\delta n(\mathbf{v}_i,\mu_\mathrm{U})$. Evaluate: 
	\begin{itemize}
		\item if $\abs{\delta n(\mathbf{v}_i,\mu_\mathrm{U})} \le \Delta n$, set $\mu_i = \mu_\mathrm{U}$ and halt;
		\item if $\delta n(\mathbf{v}_i,\mu_\mathrm{U}) > \Delta n$ do nothing;
		\item if $\delta n(\mathbf{v}_i,\mu_\mathrm{U}) < - \Delta n$, shift the upper bound up, $\mu_\mathrm{U} \to \mu_\mathrm{U} + \Delta \mu$ and repeat from step 4;
	\end{itemize}
	\item At this point, the the lower bound underestimates density while the upper bound overestimates. Start iterations:
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item Compute the midpoint,
		\[
			\mu_\mathrm{M} = \frac{\mu_\mathrm{L} + \mu_\mathrm{U}}{2}
		\]
		\item Evaluate:
		\begin{itemize}
			\item If $\abs{\delta n(\mathbf{v}_i,\mu_\mathrm{M})} \le \Delta n$, set $\mu_i = \mu_\mathrm{M}$ and halt;
			\item If $\delta n(\mathbf{v}_i,\mu_\mathrm{M}) > \Delta n$, set $\mu_\mathrm{U} = \mu_\mathrm{M}$ and repeat from point a);
			\item If $\delta n(\mathbf{v}_i,\mu_\mathrm{M}) < -\Delta n$, set $\mu_\mathrm{L} = \mu_\mathrm{M}$ and repeat from point a);
		\end{itemize}
	\end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}
The bottleneck of the procedure is our ability to compute expectation values. This depends on how much we are able to simplify analytically the computations for each phase.

As will turn out in the derivation, chemical potential will be renormalised by interaction. This means that applying self-consistent HF methods to the EHM will produce an effective hamiltonian containing a shift to the chemical potential,
\[
	\mu \to \mu + F[\mathbf{v}] \equiv \tilde{\mu} [\mathbf{v}]
\]
being $F$ some function of the HFPs. The chemical potential $\mu$ is an example of Renormalised Model Parameter (RMP).

Note that at step $i$ of the iHF algorithm, our bisection procedure determines $\tilde{\mu}_i$, not $\mu_i$. This consideration is relevant when dealing with ground state energy estimation. Let us abstract from the EHM and iHF, and consider a generic hamiltonian $\hat H$. To find the ground state $\ket{\Psi}$ means precisely to solve
\[
	\fdv{}{\bra{\Psi}} \mel{\Psi}{\hat H}{\Psi} = 0
\]
Now, working with a fixed particles number $N$ means we are performing a constrained minimisation (which is, taking the functional derivative) on the composite operator
\[
	\hat H - \mu (\hat N - N) \equiv \hat K + \mu N
\]
being $\mu$ the Lagrange multiplier, $\hat N$ the number operator and $\hat K \equiv \hat H - \mu \hat N$. Finding the constrained ground state parametrised by a vector $\mathbf{v}$ now means to solve
\[
	\mathbf{v} \implies \ket{\Psi [\mathbf{v}]}
	\quad\colon\quad
	\begin{cases}
		\displaystyle
		\fdv{}{\bra{\Psi}} \mel{\Psi}{\hat K + \mu N}{\Psi} \bigg|_{\ket{\Psi}=\ket{\Psi [\mathbf{v}]}} = 0 \\[1em]
		\mel{\Psi [\mathbf{v}]}{\hat N}{\Psi [\mathbf{v}]} = N
	\end{cases}
\]
In the first line $\mu N$ is derived to zero; the constraint is imposed in the second line. Energy is given by:
\[
\begin{aligned}
	\langle \hat H \rangle &= \mel{\Psi[\mathbf{v}]}{\hat K}{\Psi[\mathbf{v}]} + \mu N \\
	&= \Omega[\mathbf{v}] + (\tilde{\mu}[\mathbf{v}]-F[\mathbf{v}]) N
\end{aligned}
\]
being $\Omega$ the expectation value (EV) of $\hat K$. To correctly compute energy, we need to add $+\mu N$; since we determine $\tilde{\mu}[\mathbf{v}] \neq \mu$, everything needs to be expressed in terms of it. Using this relation, when the algorithm has converged, we can estimate ground-state energy.

\subsection{The convergence scheme}\label{subsec:reject-mix-iterate}

Let us discuss briefly the convergence criterion of our algorithm and the iteration mechanism. The iHF scheme we design is a sequential loop that runs up to a logical halt condition. In order to specify such condition, we need to define our notion of ``convergence''. Recall the definition of $\mathrm{A}(\mathbf{v})$, the non-linear map in HFPs space whose component are given by a set of self-consistency equations: we claim to have reached convergence when $\mathbf{v}$ comes ``close enough'' to a fixed point of the map $\mathrm{A}$, as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:hf-A-fixed-point}. Explicitly, we stop whenever the following logic condition is satisfied for each component of $\mathbf{v} = (v_1, v_2, \cdots)$:
\[
	\forall v_j
	\quad\colon\quad
	\abs{v_j-\mathrm{A}_j(\mathbf{v})} \le \Delta v_j
\]
being $\mathrm{A}(\mathbf{v})$ the map image of the HFP vector $\mathbf{v}$, and $\mathrm{A}_j$ its $j$-th component. The tolerance $\Delta v_j$ is arbitrary. If just one component of $\mathbf{v}$ is mapped ``too far'' (with respect to $\Delta v_j$) the step is rejected and iterations continue. In this text we deal in with quantities of order $10^{-1}$, so convergence values of order $10^{-3} \divisionsymbol 10^{-4}$ will be in general sufficient. 

At step $i$, we check the distance of the two vectors $\mathrm{A}(\mathbf{v}_i)$ and $\mathbf{v}_i$ and if possible, halt. If instead convergence is not reached, we need repeat the cycle with a new initialiser $\mathbf{v}_{i+1}$. A very simple choice would be to set $\mathbf{v}_{i+1} = \mathbf{v}_i$. We do something slightly different:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mix}
	\mathbf{v}_{i+1} \equiv g \mathrm{A} \left( \mathbf{v}_{i} \right) + (1-g) \mathbf{v}_{i}
\end{equation}
The ``mixing parameter'' $g$ is a simple but useful knob to tune algorithmic evolution. Its usefulness comes from this observation: even if a fixed point exists, we don't know how easy it is to actually get there following $\mathrm{A}$. Setting $g=1$, the new initialiser is just the image of the previous initialiser. By moving ``slower'' ($g<1$) with an accurate optimisation of the mixing, it is possible to reach convergence in a controlled way.

Let us give some concrete justification about the relevance of controlling $g$. When it comes to HF iterative schemes of this kind, it can sometimes happen for the algorithm to remain stuck in infinite loops. An example of this behaviour is given in Sec.~\ref{subsec:normal-g-optimization}. A typical situation is the following: during algorithm evolution we reach a couple of points $\mathbf{v}^{(a)}$, $\mathbf{v}^{(b)}$ such that
\[
	\cdots \stackrel{\mathrm{A},g}{\longrightarrow} \mathbf{v}^{(a)} \stackrel{\mathrm{A},g}{\longrightarrow} \mathbf{v}^{(b)} \stackrel{\mathrm{A},g}{\longrightarrow} \mathbf{v}^{(a)} \stackrel{\mathrm{A},g}{\longrightarrow} \mathbf{v}^{(b)} \stackrel{\mathrm{A},g}{\longrightarrow} \cdots
\]
With the notation ``$\stackrel{\mathrm{A},g}{\longrightarrow}$'' we intend one algorithmic step at mixing parameter $g$. We are stuck forever in this loop. Now, to predict if couples like $(\mathbf{v}^{(a)},\mathbf{v}^{(b)})$ exist, how they depend on $g$, where they are located in HFPs space... is impossible at this level. Thus there is no immediate way to avoid these loops \textit{a priori}. One workaround we found to work in most cases, however, was to just make $g$ smaller. We gained empirical evidence that to ``slow down'' in HFPs space makes numeric instabilities less frequent. The trade-off, however, is that if we move too slow, runtime could get very large. Note that inaccurate setup of the algorithm could lead to resources-intensive runs also in ``ordinary'' situations, when we are not stuck in a loop.

To keep the number of iterations under control, we also defined an upper bound $p$ to the total number of steps. If after $p$ steps we algorithm failed to converge, it halts. In that case, $\mathbf{v}$ is saved as a ``\texttt{NaN}'' (Not a number) vector and we move to another simulation. Introducing this simple rule, runtime is controlled and we gain an easy method to identify the regions of parameter space requiring more care -- just read from the raw data where \texttt{NaN}s have been saved and repeat simulations fine-tuning the parameters. This basically means to lower $g$ and increase $p$. These considerations end this technical introductory chapter on iHF methods.
